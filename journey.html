The Journey

Every role taught me something the next one required. This is how I got here.

01
Where it started

Teacher's Aide — IT

Gorham High School

During my junior year I became close with the administrator running the Maine Learning Technology Initiative — essentially the school's SysAdmin. Rather than sit through study halls I spent that time with him, watching and helping wherever I could. Most of my hands-on work involved swapping MacBook top cases, keyboards, and bezels, but what I was really getting was something harder to teach: a day in the life of someone in IT. The troubleshooting mindset, the methodical approach, the satisfaction of a fixed machine — I got my first real look at all of it here.

  • Learned the basics of hardware repair — disassembly, components, careful reassembly
  • Observed how a SysAdmin manages a fleet of devices at scale — imaging, deployment, monitoring
  • Understood for the first time that IT is a discipline, not just fixing things
02
First professional role

Computer Repair Technician

Bell Techlogix

After high school I landed a summer position with Bell Techlogix, which held a state contract to repair MacBooks used in the MLTI program — the same machines I had already been working on. The process was structured: pull a damaged unit off the shelf, scan it to identify the required parts, retrieve them, and perform the repair. I replaced hard drives, screens, bezels, optical drives, batteries, and occasionally full motherboards. Because of my time at Gorham High I already knew these machines well, which made the work efficient and gave me confidence early.

  • Developed speed and consistency working through a high volume of repairs
  • Learned parts identification and basic asset tracking workflows
  • Gained confidence working independently on hardware without supervision
03
Building the people side

Computer Salesman

Best Buy

Not the most glamorous role, but one of the most instructive. Helping customers find the right computer meant understanding their actual needs — not their stated specs — and translating technical decisions into language that meant something to them. This was my first real exposure to how the general public thinks about technology, and the gap between what IT professionals understand and what most people know is wide. That gap became something I paid attention to in every role that followed.

  • Learned to ask the right questions before offering solutions — listening first
  • Understood how to match technical capability to real-world need and budget
  • Developed the foundation for communicating technical concepts to non-technical people
04
Troubleshooting enters the picture

Sales Representative

T-Mobile — Boston, MA

Working in Boston pushed my soft skills further but also introduced something new: live troubleshooting. Unlike my earlier repair work where devices arrived already broken and the cause was known, customers at T-Mobile walked in off the street with a phone that wasn't working and no clear explanation of why. I had to figure out the problem, determine whether it was hardware or software, understand what the customer actually wanted to achieve, and find a path forward — often under time pressure with other customers waiting. It was also my first professional experience with mobile devices and cellular infrastructure.

  • Developed real troubleshooting instincts — diagnosing unknowns rather than known faults
  • Learned to separate the customer's described problem from the actual problem
  • First exposure to mobile devices, plans, and carrier-level infrastructure professionally
  • Strengthened composure and efficiency under pressure in a high-volume environment
05
Where the framework was built

Technical Expert

Apple — South Portland, ME

Apple is where I grew the most — both technically and professionally. I started as a sales associate, moved quickly to the Genius Bar handling appointments for Apple Watch, iPad, and iPhone, then earned a promotion to Technical Expert with hands-on hardware repair responsibilities. At the Genius Bar I was diagnosing, troubleshooting, and creating documentation for every interaction. As a Technical Expert I was performing battery, screen, and camera replacements on iPhones directly.

The most valuable thing Apple gave me was a structured troubleshooting framework: every problem falls into one of four categories — Hardware, Software, User, or Environment. That framework still shapes how I approach every issue today. Alongside that came a discipline around documentation — every note had to tell the full story of what was done, why it was done, and what the next step should be if the problem wasn't resolved.

  • Internalized the four-category troubleshooting framework: Hardware, Software, User, Environment
  • Built disciplined documentation habits — complete, clear, and useful to whoever reads it next
  • Performed hands-on hardware repairs at a professional standard under Apple's procedures
  • Assisted with new hire training as a Lead-and-Learn facilitator
  • Learned how to manage customer expectations through complex, multi-visit repair scenarios
06
First enterprise environment

PC Support Technician

Bangor Savings Bank — Portland, ME

Bangor Savings Bank was where I entered a proper enterprise IT environment for the first time. The scale was different, the stakes were different, and the toolset was different. I handled the help desk, supported users remotely through TeamViewer and RDP, managed access and password resets through Active Directory, and regularly worked on hardware in person across multiple branch locations. The exposure to Microsoft 365, enterprise ticketing systems, and asset management gave me foundational fluency that every sysadmin role builds on.

What I valued most here was the visibility into how an IT department is actually structured — how customer service, networking, systems, security, and identity and access management teams each own their slice and hand off to each other. Understanding that structure from the inside changed how I thought about escalation, documentation, and my own professional trajectory.

  • Gained hands-on experience with Active Directory — account management, access control, password policy
  • Worked daily with Microsoft 365 — Outlook, Teams, OneDrive, and desktop applications
  • Learned enterprise ticketing and asset management systems and their role in team operations
  • Understood how large IT departments are structured and how teams hand off to each other
  • Assisted with equipment procurement, configuration, and deployment for branch openings
  • Developed professional escalation instincts — knowing when to own a problem vs. when to hand it up
07
Current role

Network & Systems Administrator

Northern Human Services

This is the role everything before it was building toward. As Network & Systems Administrator I am responsible for the infrastructure that the organization depends on daily — from Microsoft 365 and Azure AD / Entra ID to endpoint management through Intune, on-premises Active Directory, virtual machines, network infrastructure, and security operations. I report directly to the CIO and operate with a scope that spans every layer of the stack.

I am currently ramping up in this role, bringing everything I have learned across every previous stop and applying it at a level of responsibility I have been working toward. The homelab I run at home is not separate from this work — it is where I test, learn, and prepare before touching anything in production.

  • Administering Microsoft 365, Azure AD / Entra ID, and hybrid identity environments
  • Managing endpoint devices through Microsoft Intune — MDM, MAM, compliance, and configuration profiles
  • Maintaining on-premises Active Directory — users, groups, OUs, GPOs, replication, and domain health
  • Deploying and managing virtual machines across VMware / Hyper-V infrastructure
  • Configuring and maintaining network infrastructure — routing, switching, firewalls, VLANs, VPNs
  • Implementing security baselines, MFA, conditional access, and data loss prevention policies
Active — ongoing
